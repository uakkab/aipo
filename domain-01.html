Foundation Layer · Domain 01
Process Maturity
From Tacit Knowledge to Autonomous Automation
Every organization runs on operating knowledge: processes, workflows, policies, and procedures. Most of it lives in people's heads. Before any AI transformation can succeed, that knowledge must move from tacit to documented to practiced to automated. This domain guides that journey across five levels of maturity.
💡 Core Principle: "Automation does not improve a process. It fixes it in place and runs it faster."
Foundation
Why Process Maturity Matters

The Core Insight

"Automation does not improve a process. It fixes it in place and runs it faster."

If a process is undefined, automation will automate whatever version the implementer happened to observe. If a process is inconsistent, automation will pick one variant and silently retire the others. If the process depends on someone quietly correcting bad inputs, automation removes that person and the corrections with them.

This is not new. Michael Hammer warned about "paving the cow paths" in 1990. What is new: AI lowers the cost of automating something to near zero. Organizations now automate things they would never have formalized before. The prerequisite work therefore matters more in the AI era, not less.

The Four-State Ladder

Operating knowledge moves through four states, one process at a time:

  1. Define it down to every detail, including exceptions
  2. Run it manually as defined, so staff build intuition for where it bends
  3. Automate it under supervision by interviewing people who hold that intuition
  4. Let it run autonomously with limited oversight

⚠️ Skipping a level is the most common cause of failed automation.

Prerequisite
The Readiness Gate: 6 Domains That Must Pass

Before any process can be automated, all six domains must be ready. A "mostly" on any of them is a "no" for that process.

1

Process

Documented, stable, measured
  • Artifact: Process exists as a document, not just in people's heads
  • Stability: Process has run in current form for at least one full business cycle
  • Volume & Cost: Known: runs per month, time per run, cost, error rate, downstream impact
  • Ownership: Single named owner accountable for definition, exceptions, and outcomes
✓ Gate: Documented including branches and exceptions; stable for at least one cycle; volume and cost known; one named owner
2

Data

Known, accessible, classified, measured
  • Inventory: You know where the data is (systems, owners, formats)
  • Source of Truth: One system wins when data disagrees
  • Machine Accessible: Not PDFs or emails; APIs, databases, or structured exports
  • Quality Measured: Completeness, accuracy, consistency known for fields the automation will use
  • Classification: Data classified at minimum as Public / Internal / Restricted
✓ Gate: Inputs machine-accessible; source of truth defined; quality measured on a sample; classification applied
3

Structure

Roles, governance, exception handling
  • Roles by Outcome: Roles defined by what they ensure, not what activities they do
  • Bridging Role: Someone owns the interface between technology and operations (with capacity)
  • Decision Rights: Clear who can approve, pause, rollback, and set exception thresholds
  • Exception Path: Defined route where exceptions land, who picks them up, how quickly
✓ Gate: Affected roles framed by outcome; bridging role staffed; decision rights settled; exception path defined
4

Technical

APIs, identity, logging, environments
  • Interfaces: Systems can be read from and written to programmatically
  • Identity & Access: Automations run as scoped identities with audit trails and revocation paths
  • Logging: Every automated action recorded with context to reconstruct what happened and why
  • Environments: A non-production place to test automation before production
  • Support: In-house or retained capacity to understand, debug, and modify automation
✓ Gate: Systems reachable programmatically; automation identity scoped and audited; logging in place; non-production environment exists
5

People

Literacy, psychological safety, capacity
  • Baseline Literacy: Staff understand what AI/automation can and cannot do (plausible vs. verified output, failure modes)
  • Psychological Safety: Established norm that surfacing problems is rewarded, not punished
  • Change Capacity: Organization not in the middle of merger, migration, crisis, or leadership transition
✓ Gate: Baseline literacy delivered; reporting norm established; no competing organizational crisis
6

Measurement

Baseline captured before automation
  • Current State: Measured values for cycle time, hands-on time, volume, error rate, and cost
  • Post-Automation: Will track exception rate, exception resolution time, and automation uptime
  • Comparison: Automation success measured against baseline, not opinion
✓ Gate: Pre-automation baseline captured (cycle time, errors, cost, hands-on time, volume)
Progression

L0: Tacit

Process exists only in people's heads. How it's done varies by person and their experience. Output quality depends entirely on the performer.

Why you're here: This is where every new process starts, and where processes remain that have never been formalized.

How to exit: Name a process owner and have them document it. Move to L1.

L1: Defined

Every step, branch, input, output, and exception is written down. A stranger to the job could theoretically follow the document. Execution is still variable in practice.

Exit criterion: Document survives review by someone who has never done the job.

How to exit: Run the process manually, exactly as documented, for one full business cycle. Record what breaks. Move to L2.

L2: Practiced

⚠️ This is the level everyone skips, and it's where most automation fails.

Process has been run as documented for at least one full cycle. Staff have built intuition for where it bends and breaks. Exceptions are known and their frequency is understood. The definition has been corrected by real use.

Exit criterion: One full business cycle run as defined; exception list is stable (no new exceptions surfacing).

Why it matters: This is where the document meets reality. It's how staff discover exceptions the author never imagined, and what makes L3 interviews worth conducting.

L3: Supervised Automation

Process is encoded in automation rules or AI system prompts. The machine executes the process. A human reviews every output before it is acted upon. Exceptions that rules cannot handle are routed to a human queue.

Exit criterion: Error rate at or below manual baseline, sustained over agreed period (typically 2-4 weeks).

How to exit: Build confidence through measurement. Once exception rates and error rates are stable and below baseline, reduce review. Move to L4.

L4: Autonomous

Machine executes with limited human oversight. Humans review samples and exceptions, not every output. Most exceptions are resolved automatically; novel ones are escalated. Process changes flow through the automation (versioned, tested).

Exit criterion: Exception rate and outcomes stable; rollback path tested and verified.

Note: L4 is not "no humans." It is humans at the edges: auditing samples, handling novel exceptions, deciding when the process itself should change.

Assessment
Domain 01 Readiness Checklist

Domain 01: Process Maturity is ready when these 4 items are true:

1

Key processes documented

Step-by-step SOPs and decision points. Every step, branch, input, output, and exception written down.

Maps to: L1 Defined minimum

2

Process owners assigned

Each process has a single named owner accountable for its definition, exceptions, and outcomes.

Maps to: L1 Defined minimum

3

Performance baselines established

Current cycle time, error rate, cost, and hands-on time are known. This is the measurement against which automation will be judged.

Maps to: L2 Practiced minimum

4

Exception paths mapped

Not just the happy path. Documented: what happens when inputs are missing, malformed, contradictory, or when the happy path does not apply.

Maps to: L2 Practiced minimum

PLUS: All six prerequisite domains must pass (Process, Data, Structure, Technical, People, Measurement). See "Readiness Gate" section above.

🚨 Common Gap Signals: Recognize When You're Not Ready

  • "Tribal knowledge is the primary process documentation" — Only the SMEs know how it really works
  • "Different teams do the same task differently" — No consistency or standardization
  • "No one can describe where a process starts and ends" — Fuzzy boundaries, unclear triggers
  • "Outcomes are measured, but the process producing them is not" — You track results but not the steps
  • "We're not sure what a bad exception looks like" — Edge cases are surprises, not managed
Common Mistakes
Anti-Patterns to Recognize and Avoid

1. Automating to Avoid Defining

"We'll let the AI figure out the process." The AI will figure out a process. It will not be yours, and you will not know what it is.

Fix: Define the process first. Automation is the third step, not the first.

2. Starting with the Hardest Process

The one with the most exceptions, most stakeholders, least documentation. Automating it fails spectacularly and kills organizational momentum.

Fix: Start with the boring one. The organization needs to learn how automation fails before it can afford an interesting failure.

3. Skipping L2 (The Manual Run)

The temptation is to go straight from a finished document to automation. But L2 is where the document meets reality: where staff discover exceptions the author never imagined.

Fix: Run the process as written for one full business cycle. Document what fails. Use that to design the automation.

4. Treating Readiness as a One-Time Assessment

Every prerequisite decays. Processes drift, data degrades, owners leave. You cannot assess once and assume you're ready forever.

Fix: The gate is re-run for each new automation, not passed once. Readiness is ongoing.

5. Tool Procurement Before Process Definition

Buying the platform before the process is defined produces a platform looking for a use case. The tooling decision is downstream, not upstream.

Fix: Define, practice, measure. Then decide on tools. Not the other way around.

6. Letting the Vendor Define Exception Thresholds

The point at which an automation should hand off to a human is a business decision, shaped by the cost of errors in your context. Defaults are set for the vendor's typical customer, not you.

Fix: You decide when exceptions escape to humans. This is a governance decision, not a technical one.

Next Steps

  1. Self-assess: Use the 4-item checklist above. Which items do you have? Which are missing?
  2. Assess readiness: Do all six prerequisite domains pass? If not, which is the biggest gap?
  3. Place your process: Use the L0-L4 matrix. Where does your process sit today?
  4. Plan the next level: What does the exit criterion for your next level require?
  5. Work the lowest row first: Across the matrix, which row has the biggest gap? Start there.
